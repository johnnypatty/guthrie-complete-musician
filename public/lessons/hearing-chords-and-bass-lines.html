Ear Training and Transcription • Weeks 1-24

Hearing Chords and Bass Lines

Stop treating a solo as notes floating over an anonymous backing. Hear where harmony moves and why a melodic note feels stable, bright, dark, tense, or resolved.

Progressive ear-training • hearing • chords • bass • lines

Purpose

Stop treating a solo as notes floating over an anonymous backing. Hear where harmony moves and why a melodic note feels stable, bright, dark, tense, or resolved.

Method

1. Bass first

Loop one phrase and sing only the lowest movement. Find roots on one string. Mark when the bass holds while the upper chord changes, or moves under a sustained chord.

2. Major/minor/other

For each root, test major and minor triads quietly. If neither fits, listen for suspended, diminished, dominant, power-chord, or color-tone possibilities. Do not add extensions before basic quality is stable.

3. Function and degree

Find the tonal center, then label roots as scale degrees/Roman numerals. Ask which chord sounds like home, departure, dominant pull, borrowed color, or unresolved motion.

4. Top note

Sing the highest obvious chord tone or melody note. Build an inversion that preserves it. This turns chord detection into voice leading rather than root-position guessing.

5. Verify by reduction

Play root plus third; then root/seventh; then a small triad. The smallest voicing that explains the sound is more informative than a six-note shape.

Daily five-minute drill

Use the offline backing player with the chord display hidden or look away. Write the roots/qualities you hear, then reveal the timeline. Next sing the third of each chord before it arrives.

Success test

For a four-to-eight chord loop, you can sing roots, identify most basic qualities, map the form, and create a melody that lands on at least one characteristic chord tone per change.

Common mistake

Assuming the lowest guitar note is always the chord root. Inversions, pedal tones, and omitted roots exist.
