Use this every time an assignment says **No Tabs**. The goal is not to prove you never need help; it is to connect hearing, understanding, and the fretboard before your eyes borrow someone else's answer.

## 1. Absorb

Listen 5–10 times without the guitar. Mark where the fragment begins/ends, its pulse, repeated ideas, and whether it feels resolved. Slow playback is allowed; visual fret videos are not.

## 2. Sing

Sing or hum the rhythm and contour. If pitch is difficult, clap the rhythm and describe “same/up/down/leap.” Do not proceed until the phrase exists away from the instrument.

## 3. Establish context

Find the pulse and likely tonal center. Listen for bass/root movement. Ask whether the phrase begins before, on, or after the beat and whether notes belong to the chord or pull toward it.

## 4. Loop microscopically

Work with 1–4 seconds. Locate the first reliable note, then compare the next note by direction and distance. Avoid random fretboard searching: sing, predict, test.

## 5. Write your map

Use note names or scale degrees, approximate rhythm, bar/beat position, articulation, and chord underneath. This is analysis, not copied tab.

## 6. Join and test

Connect two tiny fragments. Start from several points. Play with the recording, then from memory without it. Preserve bends, slides, muting, dynamics, and rests.

## 7. Record your version

Record before checking any transcription. Compare timing, pitch, contour, register, and articulation. Fix one class of error at a time.

## 8. Transform

Create three versions:

- same notes, new rhythm;
- same rhythm, new notes that fit another chord;
- same principle in another key/neck area.

## 9. Optional verification

Only now compare a trusted tab, notation, live video, or isolated track. Differences may reveal a wrong note—or a valid alternative fingering. Keep your ear map and annotate the discovery.

## Success test

You can sing, play, explain the rhythm/context, start mid-phrase, and create a response without the recording.

## Common mistake

Looping a longer passage until the fingers mimic it while the ear still cannot sing it.
