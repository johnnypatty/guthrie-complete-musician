## Purpose

Make pick attack, fretting contact, release, and muting arrive as one rhythmic event across strings.

## Method

### 1. One-note rhythm

On one muted or fretted note, play eighths, triplets, and sixteenths. Accents must move while the hand motion remains small. This separates picking rhythm from left-hand complexity.

### 2. Fretting-hand release

Use two notes on one string. Listen for the space between them: no overlap, accidental pull-off, or squeeze. Repeat legato-only, pick-only, then synchronized.

### 3. Crossing laboratory

Use a tiny cell crossing two strings. Practise four versions: starting down/up and moving lower-to-higher/higher-to-lower. Do not assume the version that fails needs more force; examine pick depth, slant/escape, and muting.

### 4. Accent map

Play a continuous six-note or seven-note cell while accents remain in groups of four. Then keep the notes and accent every 3, 5, or 7. This separates phrase grouping from hand pattern.

### 5. Musical application

Create a two-bar line that includes one repeated note, one string crossing, one position shift, one rest, and a strong chord-tone landing. Vary dynamics on the repeat.

## Alternate versus economy picking

Learn both as tools. Alternate picking gives a consistent rhythmic mechanism. Economy picking can join sweep-like string changes efficiently. Label which you intend before judging the result. An accidental mixture that cannot be repeated is not yet a technique.

## Success test

- Every note center aligns with the subdivision grid.
- The first note after a shift/crossing is not louder or late.
- Unused strings remain quiet under a moderately gained tone.
- You can start the phrase on an upstroke without panic.

## Common mistake

Watching only the fretting hand. Many “weak finger” problems begin with inconsistent pick depth or attack timing.
