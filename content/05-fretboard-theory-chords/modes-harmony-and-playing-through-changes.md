## Modal playing: center plus characteristic tone

Do not merely run the parent major scale from a different note. Establish the modal root with a drone/vamp and feature the identifying degree:

- Dorian: minor with natural 6;
- Phrygian: minor with b2;
- Lydian: major with #4;
- Mixolydian: major with b7.

Create a two-chord vamp that preserves the center and reveals the characteristic tone. Sing that tone against the root before improvising.

## Key-center playing versus chord-change playing

- **Key center:** one collection explains several chords; good for coherent melody.
- **Chord changes:** each chord's 3rd, 7th, and color tones guide phrase destinations.
- Strong improvisation moves between both views rather than naming a new scale every beat.

## Target hierarchy

On a change, choose in this order:

1. common tone for continuity;
2. nearest 3rd/7th for clear harmony;
3. extension for color;
4. chromatic approach that resolves on time.

## Secondary dominants

A dominant chord may temporarily point to a diatonic chord other than tonic. In F major, A7 strongly points to Dm because C# pulls to D. Hear the altered note as direction, not a theory exception.

## Modal interchange

Borrow a chord from the parallel major/minor or another parallel mode for color. In a major key, bVII, iv minor, or bVI may create familiar rock/cinematic movement. Identify the borrowed chord's surprising notes and use them deliberately in melody.

## Practice progression

Use Fmaj7–A7–Dm7–Eb–F6–Bbmaj7–Gdim7–Am7–Dm7–Gm7–C7–F.

1. Play roots only and hear form.
2. Add 3rds/7ths.
3. Voice-lead upper triads.
4. Improvise one key-center pass.
5. Improvise targeting each change.
6. Combine: singable motifs plus only the targets needed to clarify changes.

## Success test

Listeners can hear harmony change through your line even without chord accompaniment, but the solo still sounds like connected speech rather than an arpeggio demonstration.
