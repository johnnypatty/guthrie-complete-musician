The neck is not five boxes. It is one pitch map viewed through intervals, chord tones, and sound.

## Phase 1 — natural-note landmarks

For one week at a time, emphasize two strings. Say a random natural note, name the string, and locate it within five seconds. Use fret 0–12 first; fret 12–24 repeats the pitch-class pattern.

Anchors:

- open-string names and fret 12 octaves;
- E/F and B/C are adjacent semitones;
- every other natural-note pair has one fret between;
- octave shapes verify, but do not replace naming.

## Phase 2 — interval from any root

Pick a random root anywhere. Sing and find: b2, 2, b3, 3, 4, #4/b5, 5, b6, 6, b7, 7. Say the interval before moving. Check with the offline chord/scale reference.

## Phase 3 — chord-tone map

Over one chord, locate only roots. Next only thirds, then fifths/sevenths. Improvise with one target family at a time. This converts note knowledge into harmonic timing.

## Phase 4 — horizontal and diagonal playing

- One-string melody: prevents box reflex.
- Two adjacent strings: emphasizes interval choices.
- Diagonal connection: follow a sung contour through positions.
- Position restriction: forces inversions and register awareness.

## Timed test

Generate 12 random note/string requests. Score response time and errors. Goal by week 12: most natural notes in under three seconds; by week 24: natural/accidental targets plus a named chord relationship in under five seconds.

## Success test

You can find a target note in three regions, sing its function over the chord, and approach it from above/below in an improvised phrase.

## Common mistake

Running a memorized scale while claiming to search for a note. Stop, name, hear, then move.
